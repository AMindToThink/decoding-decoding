\title{Modeling and Calibrating: LLMs Figure out their Decoding Process}
\author{Matthew Khoriaty}
\date{Project write-up, \emph{Reliable ML with Unreliable Black Boxes}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
An autoregressive language model is trained to predict text, so when it
generates text it has an incentive to model its own generation process ---
including the decoder that samples from its logits. We accumulate evidence that
models do exactly this: they infer features of their own decoder (a token
blacklist, a top-$k$ truncation, a temperature) from the text in their context,
and shift their predictions accordingly. We build a streaming Bayesian filter
that estimates the model's inferred inverse-temperature $\hat\beta_t$ online and
a corrector that divides a user's target by $\hat\beta_t$. An empirical sweep
shows the corrector pins the effective temperature in the ``boredom'' regime
($\beta^{\mathrm{target}}>1$) but overshoots in the ``confusion'' regime
($\beta^{\mathrm{target}}<1$). Probing what the filter measures sharpens the
picture and revises the original framing: on real text the filter is
\emph{not} an online version of Guo temperature scaling (it sits
$\sim\!1$ nat in $\log\hat\beta$ away from the post-hoc MLE, e.g.\
$\calLogBetaStreamPooledWT$ vs $\calLogBetaMLEWT$ on WikiText), and \dubious[The question isn't whether qwen can handle natural english like wikitext, but whether it can handle other text, like the creative writing on which we found it miscalibrated]{Qwen-2.5-3B
is already well calibrated on naturalistic English}. A controlled
sampler-switch experiment falsifies the strong hypothesis that the belief is a
fast posterior read off per-token entropy. \dubious[Rank statistics and entropy are super closely related. This claim rubs me the wrong way. We know *something* is happening, and our first thoughts at nailing it down didn't work out perfectly, but that doesn't imply that things we can't test properly are the right answer. Do you get what I'm saying?]{The belief that survives is slower
and more structural --- carried by rank statistics and blacklist signatures,
not instantaneous entropy.} \dubious[Didn't the stability come from crushing the temperature to zero? That's not calibration.]{Finally, two metrics for the corrector disagree:
closed-loop stability prefers full correction, while invariance on held-out
natural text shows full correction is catastrophic unless the filter is given a
fading memory ($\gamma<1$)}. All numbers and tables in this document are
generated from the experiments' data files by build scripts; the prose cites
them by name.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
An autoregressive language model emits, at each step, a probability
distribution over next tokens given the prefix so far. \emph{Decoding} is the
choice of how to sample from that distribution: temperature scaling, top-$k$,
and top-$p$ all act by sharpening or flattening the model's distribution before
drawing the next token. This project starts from a question about what a
language model ``thinks'' about its own decoding process. Because models are
trained for text prediction, not generation, they have an incentive to model
their own generation process. We hypothesize, and have begun to validate, that
\emph{a language model forms a belief about its own decoding parameters by
conditioning on the text it has generated so far}. If you sample at a low
temperature, the model notices that the recent text is low-entropy and adjusts
its output probabilities to match; the low-temperature samples then further
condition the model to expect low-temperature text. This is a feedback loop.

Mirostat~\citep{basu2021mirostat} documented a version of this effect and
patched it with a control loop on aggregate per-token surprise. That fix cannot
adapt to context, because high- or low-entropy text is sometimes exactly what a
response calls for. The broader and (to us) more interesting question is to
\emph{model} what an LLM believes about its own decoder. This is close to the
calibration literature --- post-hoc temperature scaling~\citep{guo2017calibration},
its use on language-model surprisal~\citep{liu2024temperature}, and trained
universal calibrators~\citep{shen2024thermometer} --- and to the
algorithms-with-predictions viewpoint~\citep{mitzenmacher2020algorithms} of the
course, in which a possibly-unreliable prediction (here, the model's inferred
decoder) augments a downstream decision. It also connects to recent work on
online and high-dimensional
calibration~\citep{peng2025calibration,fishelson2025calibration}.

\paragraph{Contributions and revisions.}
The original proposal framed the streaming estimator as ``online temperature
scaling converging in the large-data limit to the same global $T$ that Guo's
batch MLE fits.'' This write-up reports the experiments we ran to test that
framing, and the framing does not fully survive. We find: (i) positive,
model-internal evidence that the model infers its decoder
(\S\ref{sec:positive}); (ii) a corrector that works asymmetrically
(\S\ref{sec:corrector}); (iii) that the filter is \emph{not} Guo MLE on real
text and that the well-calibrated-model regime leaves no post-hoc temperature
gain (\S\ref{sec:measure}); (iv) that the belief is not a fast entropy read-out
(\S\ref{sec:form}); and (v) that two natural criteria for tuning the corrector
disagree, with memory decay as the load-bearing parameter (\S\ref{sec:dynamics}).
The belief-imprint hypothesis is \emph{refined and constrained}, not refuted.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
% =====================================================================
For readers new to them, this section glosses the concepts and tools the paper
uses, grouped by where they first appear. Each term gets a one- or two-sentence
plain-English definition and a footnote pointing to a focused explainer.

\paragraph{Language models and decoding.}
An \textbf{autoregressive language model} emits, at each position, a probability
distribution over the next token given all preceding
tokens.\footnote{E.~Voita, ``Language Modeling,'' \emph{NLP Course For You}:
\url{https://lena-voita.github.io/nlp_course/language_modeling.html}.} That
distribution comes from a \textbf{softmax}, which turns a vector of real-valued
scores (the \emph{logits} $\ell$) into positive numbers that sum to
one.\footnote{E.~Bendersky, ``The Softmax function and its derivative'':
\url{https://eli.thegreenplace.net/2016/the-softmax-function-and-its-derivative/}.}
\textbf{Decoding} is how we sample from it: \textbf{temperature} rescales the
logits before the softmax (low temperature sharpens the distribution, high
temperature flattens it), \textbf{top-$k$} keeps only the $k$ most probable
tokens, and \textbf{top-$p$} (nucleus) keeps the smallest set of tokens whose
probability mass exceeds $p$.\footnote{M.~Labonne, ``Decoding Strategies in Large
Language Models,'' Hugging Face blog:
\url{https://huggingface.co/blog/mlabonne/decoding-strategies}.} We parameterize
temperature by its reciprocal, the \textbf{inverse temperature} $\beta=1/T$:
multiplying the logits by $\beta>1$ concentrates the distribution and $\beta<1$
spreads it out. \textbf{Mirostat} \citep{basu2021mirostat} and post-hoc
\textbf{temperature scaling} \citep{guo2017calibration} are prior methods that tune
a single temperature to control surprise or to calibrate confidence.

\paragraph{The filter's machinery (\S\ref{sec:method}).}
\textbf{Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)} picks the parameter value that makes
the observed data most probable.\footnote{StatQuest (J.~Starmer), ``Maximum
Likelihood, Clearly Explained'': \url{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XepXtl9YKwc}.}
The \textbf{score} is the gradient of the log-likelihood with respect to the
parameter (it is zero at the MLE), and the \textbf{Fisher information} is its
curvature --- how sharply the log-likelihood peaks, equivalently how much one
observation tells you about the parameter.\footnote{Score and Fisher information,
UC~Berkeley Stat~210A lecture notes:
\url{https://stat210a.berkeley.edu/fall-2024/reader/score-fisher.html}; and
A.~Kristiadi, ``Fisher Information Matrix'':
\url{https://agustinus.kristia.de/blog/fisher-information/}.}
\textbf{Newton's method} updates a parameter by $\theta \leftarrow \theta -
\text{slope}/\text{curvature}$, i.e.\ score over Fisher.\footnote{``Taylor Series
and Newton's Method,'' suzyahyah.github.io:
\url{https://suzyahyah.github.io/calculus/optimization/2018/04/06/Taylor-Series-Newtons-Method.html}.}
The \textbf{Laplace approximation} replaces a complicated posterior distribution
with a Gaussian centred at its peak (the mode), with width set by the Fisher
curvature there --- this is what turns the belief update into a cheap, closed-form
step.\footnote{A.~Kristiadi, Laplace-approximation tutorial:
\url{https://agustinus.kristia.de/blog/laplace/}.} Performing that update in the
score-over-Fisher metric is exactly \textbf{natural gradient} (Fisher scoring): a
gradient step preconditioned by the Fisher information.\footnote{A.~Kristiadi,
natural-gradient-descent tutorial:
\url{https://agustinus.kristia.de/blog/natural-gradient/}.} The filter places a
\textbf{log-normal prior} on $\beta$ (a bell curve on $\log\beta$, so $\beta>0$
with mean $1$) and performs one such Laplace/Newton step per token --- a streaming
\textbf{Bayesian posterior update}.

\paragraph{Evaluation and information (\S\ref{sec:measure},~\S\ref{sec:form}).}
\textbf{Cross-entropy} measures how surprised a model is by held-out text (lower is
better); \textbf{perplexity} is its exponential, and both are reported here in
\textbf{nats} (natural-log units; $1$ nat $=1/\!\ln 2 \approx 1.44$
bits).\footnote{``Understanding Evaluation Metrics for Language Models,'' \emph{The
Gradient}:
\url{https://thegradient.pub/understanding-evaluation-metrics-for-language-models/}.}
The \textbf{negative log-likelihood (NLL) surface} is cross-entropy plotted as a
function of $\beta$; a key fact in \S\ref{sec:measure} is that it is nearly flat
near its minimum. \textbf{Entropy} (also in nats) measures how spread-out a
distribution is --- largest for a uniform distribution and near zero when one token
dominates.

\paragraph{Linear algebra and statistics (\S\ref{sec:positive},~\S\ref{sec:corrector}).}
The \textbf{singular value decomposition (SVD)} factorizes a matrix into ranked,
orthogonal directions; the share of variance lying \emph{outside} the top direction
(the off-axis residual) measures how far the data depart from a single rank-one
pattern.\footnote{G.~Gundersen, ``Singular Value Decomposition as Simply as
Possible'': \url{https://gregorygundersen.com/blog/2018/12/10/svd/}.} A
\textbf{bootstrap confidence interval} estimates uncertainty by resampling the data
with replacement many times;\footnote{StatQuest (J.~Starmer), ``Bootstrapping Main
Ideas'': \url{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xz0x-8-cgaQ}.} the \textbf{cluster
bootstrap} resamples whole groups (here, prompts) rather than individual
observations, to respect within-group correlation.\footnote{``The Cluster
Bootstrap,'' R-bloggers:
\url{https://www.r-bloggers.com/2013/01/the-cluster-bootstrap/}.} A \textbf{paired
$t$-test} checks whether the mean of within-pair differences (e.g.\ the same prompt
under two conditions) is non-zero.\footnote{``The Paired t-Test,'' JMP Statistics
Knowledge Portal:
\url{https://www.jmp.com/en/statistics-knowledge-portal/t-test/paired-t-test}.}

\paragraph{Closed-loop dynamics (\S\ref{sec:corrector},~\S\ref{sec:dynamics}).}
\textbf{Teacher forcing} runs a model over fixed ground-truth text rather than over
its own samples, isolating its predictions from its
generation.\footnote{J.~Brownlee, ``What is Teacher Forcing for Recurrent Neural
Networks?'':
\url{https://machinelearningmastery.com/teacher-forcing-for-recurrent-neural-networks/}.}
The filter's \textbf{memory decay} $\gamma$ is an exponential moving average:
recent tokens count fully while older ones fade, giving an effective window of about
$1/(1-\gamma)$ tokens.\footnote{``Exponentially Weighted Moving Average,''
terbe.dev: \url{https://terbe.dev/blog/posts/exponentially-weighted-moving-average}.}
A \textbf{closed-loop} experiment lets the model condition on its own corrected
samples (a feedback loop), whereas a teacher-forced \textbf{invariance} test grades
the corrector on text it did not generate.

\section{Method: a streaming Laplace filter on $\log\beta$}\label{sec:method}
% =====================================================================
Let $\ell_t \in \mathbb{R}^V$ be the model's pre-decoding logits at step $t$ and
$x_t$ the token actually emitted. We posit a matched-prior \emph{imprint model}:
the model behaves as if the natural-reference logits are $\ell^*_t = \ell_t /
\hat\beta_t$, i.e.\ the observed logits are the reference scaled by the model's
current belief $\hat\beta_t$ about its own inverse temperature. Placing a
log-normal prior on $\beta$ (mean $1$, scale $\sigma_0$) and doing a per-token
Laplace (Newton) update of the posterior on $\eta=\log\beta$ gives a streaming
estimator whose update uses the score and Fisher information of the categorical
likelihood under $q=\mathrm{softmax}(\ell_t)$:
\[
  \text{score}_t = \ell_t[x_t] - \mathbb{E}_q[\ell_t],
  \qquad
  J_t = \tfrac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \textstyle\sum_{\tau\le t}\mathrm{Var}_q[\ell_\tau].
\]
Crucially $q$, $\mathbb{E}_q[\ell_t]$, and $\mathrm{Var}_q[\ell_t]$ are
evaluated at $\beta=1$ and never re-evaluated at the running $\hat\beta_t$: the
imprint model is defined precisely so the score and Fisher are
$\hat\beta$-independent. This makes the filter a per-token Bayesian update under
the imprint model, \emph{not} an iterated Newton solver on a global likelihood
--- a distinction that becomes important in \S\ref{sec:measure}. Three knobs
parameterize the belief dynamics: an evidence weight $\alpha$ (per-step step
size), a memory decay $\gamma$ (exponential forgetting; $\gamma=1$ is no decay,
$\gamma<1$ is a fading window of effective length $1/(1-\gamma)$), and a
correction exponent $p$.

If the imprint is a single scalar rescaling, dividing the user's target by the
estimate yields a corrector that pins the effective sampling distribution to
target:
\[
  \beta^{\mathrm{dec}}_t = \beta^{\mathrm{target}} / \hat\beta_t^{\,p},
  \qquad p=1\ \text{(full)},\quad p=0\ \text{(off)},\quad p\in(0,1)\ \text{(partial)}.
\]
Everything below uses Qwen-2.5-3B (base, fp16). The filter implementation and
its unit tests (shift-invariance, batched\,=\,serial equivalence, recovery of
$\beta_{\mathrm{true}}$ under matched-prior synthetic data) live in
\texttt{src/decoding\_decoding/counter\_decode.py}.

\subsection{A distribution-free certificate for the grid variant}
\label{sec:certificate}
The Laplace filter is a point estimator; a companion \emph{grid mixture}
variant carries worst-case guarantees. Fix a grid $\beta_1 < \dots < \beta_G$
and treat each $\beta_g$ as an expert forecasting
$P_g(x_t) = \mathrm{softmax}(\beta_g\,\ell_t)[x_t]$ on the \emph{raw} logits
(no imprint rescaling --- fixed experts are what make the guarantees hold).
Let $L_n(\beta) = \sum_{t\le n} \big[\mathrm{lse}(\beta\ell_t) -
\beta\,\ell_t[x_t]\big]$ with $\mathrm{lse} = \log\sum_v \exp(\cdot)$, let
$\pi_0$ be a prior on the grid, and let the mixture forecast token $x_t$ with
probability $\sum_g w_g(t)\,P_g(x_t)$, where $w_g(t) \propto
\pi_0[g]\,e^{-L_{t-1}(\beta_g)}$ is the Bayes posterior. This is Vovk's
Aggregating Algorithm for log loss \citep{vovk1998game,
cesabianchi2006prediction}, and the following holds for \emph{every} token
sequence --- no assumption that tokens were sampled from any $P_\beta$, or
from anything at all.

\begin{lemma}[per-sequence certificate]\label{lem:certificate}
For any logit sequence $\ell_{1:n}$ and any token sequence $x_{1:n}$:
(i)~the mixture's cumulative log loss is
$-\ln \sum_g \pi_0[g]\, e^{-L_n(\beta_g)} \le L_n(\beta_g) +
\ln(1/\pi_0[g])$ for every $g$; with uniform $\pi_0$ the regret against the
best grid temperature in hindsight is at most $\ln G$.
(ii)~With uniform $\pi_0$ the posterior mode equals
$\arg\min_g L_n(\beta_g)$, the best-fit (\emph{effective}) inverse
temperature on the grid, exactly.
(iii)~$L_n$ is convex with $L_n''(\beta) = \sum_t
\mathrm{Var}_{q_{\beta,t}}[\ell_t]$ where $q_{\beta,t} =
\mathrm{softmax}(\beta\ell_t)$, so for any $c > 0$ the sub-level set
$\{\beta : L_n(\beta) \le \min L_n + c\}$ is an interval containing the
sequence MLE; its endpoints, read off the stored grid loss profile, are a
data-dependent error bar that needs no distributional assumptions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i)~The mixture's per-step log probabilities telescope:
$\sum_t \ln \sum_g w_g(t) P_g(x_t) = \ln \sum_g \pi_0[g]\,
e^{-L_n(\beta_g)} \ge \ln\big(\pi_0[g]\, e^{-L_n(\beta_g)}\big)$ for each
$g$. (ii)~Immediate from $w_g(n) \propto e^{-L_n(\beta_g)}$.
(iii)~Each summand of $L_n$ is a $\mathrm{lse}$ of an affine function of
$\beta$ minus an affine function, hence convex; differentiate twice.
\end{proof}

``Distribution-free'' is doing double duty here, and both senses hold. The
first is over the data process: the lemma assumes nothing about how $x_{1:n}$
was produced. The second is over the estimator's own modeling assumptions:
with uniform $\pi_0$ the posterior mode in (ii) \emph{is} the grid MLE --- the
log-normal prior of the Laplace filter does no work --- and the sub-level set
in (iii) is a profile-likelihood interval, computed from the exact grid
posterior rather than a Gaussian (Laplace) approximation. The prior survives
only as a constant: the default threshold $c = \ln G$ is the uniform-prior
regret budget, and a different $\pi_0$ rescales the bar's threshold, never its
validity. What remains is a support assumption --- the grid spans a bounded
range of $\log\beta$ at finite resolution --- the far weaker residue of the
log-normal's tails.

Three honest caveats. First, the certificate is relative to the in-hindsight
best-fit $\beta$, \emph{not} the dial a counterparty actually set; under
misspecified decoders (\S\ref{sec:positive}'s blacklist and top-$k$, the
closed loop of \S\ref{sec:corrector}) no single ``true'' $\beta$ describes
the stream, and the best-fit value is precisely what ``effective
temperature'' should mean there. Second, the interval in (iii) widens
exactly when peaked logits make $\beta$ unidentifiable
($\mathrm{Var}_{q}[\ell_t] \to 0$); forecasting regret and estimation
precision degrade in opposite directions. Third, the guarantee attaches to
the mixture, not to the Laplace point filter: proper online predictors for
logistic-type losses face an exp-concavity obstruction that improper mixture
forecasts avoid \citep{foster2018logistic}, so transfer of the certificate
to the Laplace estimates is an empirical claim (tested, with thresholds
pre-registered, in \texttt{paper/grid\_certificate\_prereg.md}). A
fixed-share variant \citep{herbster1998tracking} tracks a drifting $\beta$
with regret $\approx m\ln(Gn/m)$ against any $m$-segment comparator, but
the estimation reading (ii)--(iii) is for the static case only.

\section{The model infers its decoder: positive evidence}\label{sec:positive}
% =====================================================================
Before modeling the belief, we establish that there is one. Two controlled
experiments isolate the model's response to a known decoder manipulation,
holding content fixed and comparing treatment to control with cluster-bootstrap
confidence intervals.

\paragraph{Blacklist self-prediction.}
When the $k$ highest-probability tokens are banned (set to zero probability) at
sampling time, the model's own softmax probability on the banned token drops
between early and late sequence positions --- it learns its blacklist from the
absent-token signature. For the rank-1 ban (the token `\,the'), the late-minus-early
within-treatment change is $\Delta p_{\mathrm{late-early}} = \blRankOneDeltaP$,
and the paired late treatment-vs-control difference is $\blRankOneTvc$ with 95\%
CI $[\blRankOneTvcLo,\,\blRankOneTvcHi]$, clear of zero
(Table~\ref{tab:blacklist}, Fig.~\ref{fig:blacklist}).

\section{The corrector and concentration dynamics}\label{sec:corrector}
% =====================================================================
We next put the filter in a closed loop. Self-generating $200$ tokens per
prompt at six values of $\beta^{\mathrm{target}}$, we compare an
\emph{uncorrected} arm (fixed $\beta^{\mathrm{dec}}=\beta^{\mathrm{target}}$)
against a \emph{corrected} arm ($\beta^{\mathrm{dec}}_t =
\beta^{\mathrm{target}}/\hat\beta_t$).

\paragraph{Concentration drift.}
The uncorrected arm sharpens for $\beta^{\mathrm{target}}>1$ (a ``boredom
trap'') and flattens for $\beta^{\mathrm{target}}<1$ (a ``confusion trap'');
the corrected arm keeps the sampling distribution bounded in both directions
(Table~\ref{tab:f0}, Fig.~\ref{fig:f0}). The $\beta^{\mathrm{target}}=1$ row is
a negative control, where the two arms coincide.

\paragraph{An asymmetry.} The corrector is not symmetric. In the boredom regime
it pins concentration near its $t=0$ value, as the theory predicts. In the
confusion regime it only partially works: the corrected late max-$P_{\text{sample}}$
at $\beta^{\mathrm{target}}=0.5$ settles at $\fzeroMaxPcorrHalf$, well above
where it starts --- an upward drift the strict (flat-in-$t$) prediction does
not allow. The mechanism is visible in the filter: under uncorrected
$\beta^{\mathrm{dec}}=0.5$, the streaming estimate runs to $\log\hat\beta =
\fzeroLogbhatHalf$ by $t=200$ (Table~\ref{tab:f12}), an overshoot. Dividing the
target by an overshooting $\hat\beta$ produces an over-large
$\beta^{\mathrm{dec}}$, hence the residual sharpening.

\section{What does the filter actually measure?}\label{sec:measure}
% =====================================================================
The corrector's confusion-regime failure motivates asking what the filter's
$\hat\beta$ is. We test the proposal's claim --- that it is online Guo
temperature scaling --- directly, and find it false on real text.

\paragraph{No filter pathology under self-sampling.}
First we rule out the weakest alternative: that the filter drifts on its own.
Feeding the filter i.i.d.\ samples from $\mathrm{softmax}(\ell_t)$ at
$\beta^{\mathrm{dec}}=1$ (the no-imprint null), $\log\hat\beta$ flatlines ---
median $\logBetaFinalMedWikiSelf$ on WikiText self-generation and
$\logBetaFinalMedWPSelf$ on WritingPrompts, both within one standard error of
zero (Fig.~\ref{fig:ntf}). On real Wikipedia continuations the filter agrees
with self-sampling (paired $t=\pairedTStatWiki$), but on real WritingPrompts it
drifts negative: a paired difference of $\pairedMeanDiffWP$ in $\log\beta$
($t=\pairedTStatWP$, $p<10^{-3}$). So the model is well calibrated on Wikipedia
and slightly over-confident on Reddit creative writing --- a real out-of-domain
calibration offset, but an order of magnitude smaller than the drift seen under
extreme $\beta$-decoding. The closed-loop drift is not a filter artifact.

\paragraph{The streaming filter is not Guo MLE (Exp A).}
On a proper per-corpus calibration split, the Guo MLE sits essentially at
$\log\beta=0$ on both corpora ($\calLogBetaMLEWT$ on WikiText,
$\calLogBetaMLEWP$ on WritingPrompts), confirming Qwen-2.5-3B is already
calibrated. The pooled streaming filter on the \emph{same} tokens settles at
$\log\hat\beta=\calLogBetaStreamPooledWT$ (WikiText) and
$\calLogBetaStreamPooledWP$ (WritingPrompts) --- about a nat away
(Table~\ref{tab:expa-beta}, Fig.~\ref{fig:expa-nll}). The two estimators have
\emph{different fixed points}: the filter is a single damped Newton step from
the prior mode under the imprint model, accurate near $\beta=1$ but biased as it
walks up the flat NLL surface, whereas the Guo MLE is the global minimizer.
Applied as a calibrator the filter \emph{hurts}: on WritingPrompts test, the
per-passage streaming $\hat\beta$ raises per-token PPL from $\calTestPPLWPTOne$ to
$\calTestPPLWPWPstreammed$ ($\Delta\log\mathrm{PPL}=\calTestDeltaLogPPLWPWPstreammed$,
$t=\calTestTStatWPWPstreammed$), while the Guo MLE is statistically
indistinguishable from $T=1$ (paired $t=\calTestTStatWPWPMLE$, $p=\calTestPValWPWPMLE$).
The proposal's asymptotic-equivalence claim does not survive; ``calibration''
and ``imprint detection'' are distinct targets.

\section{Recovering a known decoder temperature}\label{sec:recovery}
% =====================================================================
\S\ref{sec:measure} showed the streaming filter is not Guo's batch MLE on real
text --- but that comparison collapses the filter to a single scalar and applies
it as a \emph{global} temperature, discarding its one real selling point: a
\emph{per-token} estimate read causally off the prefix. We test that directly in
a controlled setting where the ground truth is known.

\paragraph{A faithful (re-linearised) filter.}
The production update (\S\ref{sec:method}) evaluates the score and Fisher once at
$\beta=1$ and never re-evaluates, so $\hat\beta$ is a single damped Newton step
from the prior mode --- accurate only near $\beta=1$ and biased away from it. For
the tilted family $P(x\mid\eta)=\mathrm{softmax}(e^{\eta}\ell)[x]$ the exact
score and expected Fisher in $\eta=\log\beta$ carry chain-rule factors the
original drops: $\mathrm{score}_\eta=\hat\beta\,(\ell_x-\mathbb{E}_{q_{\hat\beta}}[\ell])$
and $\mathcal{I}_\eta=\hat\beta^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}_{q_{\hat\beta}}[\ell]$, with
$q_{\hat\beta}=\mathrm{softmax}(\hat\beta\,\ell)$. The \emph{faithful} filter
re-linearises at the running $\hat\beta$ each step; over a stream it is a
consistent estimator of $\beta$, where the original is not.

\paragraph{Setup.}
For each $T\in\{0.5,0.7,1.0,1.5,2.0\}$ we seed $\scNseq$ sequences with a short
real prefix ($\scSeedLen$ tokens, WikiText$+$WritingPrompts) and sample $\scGenLen$
continuation tokens from $\mathrm{softmax}((1/T)\,\ell_t)$, so the decoder
inverse-temperature is a \emph{known} $\beta=1/T$. We run both filters over the
generated tokens (warmup $\scWarmup$, $\sigma_0=\scSigmaZero$) and, causally, use
the pre-update $\hat\beta_t$ as a per-token predictive temperature
$\tau_t=\hat\beta_t$; NLL is computed exactly. A pooled-MLE sanity check recovers
$1/T$ at every $T$ (Table~\ref{tab:recovery}).

\paragraph{Result.}
The faithful filter \emph{recovers the imposed temperature} from the prefix
alone: its settled $\log\hat\beta$ sits within $0.10$ of the truth across the
range (bias $\scFaithBiasZeroSeven$ at $T{=}0.7$, $\scFaithBiasTwo$ at $T{=}2.0$),
while the original is badly biased --- it overshoots for $\beta>1$ (bias
$\scOrigBiasZeroSeven$ at $T{=}0.7$) and diverges for $\beta<1$ (bias
$\scOrigBiasTwo$ at $T{=}2.0$). Applied as a causal per-token temperature, the
faithful estimate \emph{beats the default $\beta{=}1$ model} on held-out NLL by
$\scNllGainZeroSeven$ nats at $T{=}0.7$ (paired $t=\scNllGainTZeroSeven$) and
$\scNllGainTwo$ nats at $T{=}2.0$ ($t=\scNllGainTTwo$), landing within
$\scOracleGapTwo$ nats of the oracle (the known-optimal global $1/T$); at $T{=}1$,
where nothing needs correcting, it costs only $\scNllGainOne$ nats. \emph{Honest
caveat:} for $T\ge1.5$ the generated text is near-random (generating-distribution
entropy $\scGenEntropyOneFive$ and $\scGenEntropyTwo$ nats), so those largest
gains calibrate incoherent text; the coherent, sharper-than-default regimes
($T=0.5,0.7$) still show clean, significant wins. This is a demonstration that the
corrected filter recovers and exploits a decoder temperature from context alone
--- not a claim that natural text carries such a recoverable temperature
(\S\ref{sec:survey} tests exactly that).

\section{Is natural text mis-temperatured? A calibration survey}\label{sec:survey}
% =====================================================================
Claim of \S\ref{sec:recovery}: the filter \emph{can} exploit a decoder temperature
when one is present. Does natural text present one? We fit the optimal global
temperature (Guo MLE on a train split) per text type and measure its held-out NLL
gain over $\beta=1$, across $\scSurvNDatasets$ types spanning in-distribution to
far-OOD ($\scSurvNtrain$ train / $\scSurvNtest$ test passages each, length
$\scSurvLength$; Table~\ref{tab:survey}).

\paragraph{The model is remarkably temperature-calibrated everywhere.}
Optimal $T$ lies in $[\,\scSurvOptTRecaman,\,\scSurvMaxOptT\,]$ across all types, and the best
global temperature buys at most $\scSurvGainRecaman$ nats on any \emph{real} type
(only $\scSurvGainShuffledWikitext$ nats even on token-shuffled gibberish, the
extreme control). The OOD/atypical types are \emph{statistically} mis-temperatured
in a sensible direction --- mildly overconfident on symbolic and non-Latin text
(ABC music $T=\scSurvOptTAbcMusic$, $\Delta$NLL $\scSurvGainAbcMusic$,
$t=\scSurvGainTAbcMusic$; Telugu $T=\scSurvOptTTelugu$) --- but the magnitudes are
practically negligible. Difficulty does not imply mis-temperature: Yoruba is the
hardest natural text ($\beta{=}1$ NLL $\scSurvNllBetaOneYoruba$) yet
$T=\scSurvOptTYoruba$.

\paragraph{Recam\'an (A005132) is the instructive exception.}
On this deterministic-but-erratic integer sequence the model is extremely confident
and usually right ($\beta{=}1$ NLL $\scSurvNllBetaOneRecaman$, entropy
$\scSurvEntropyRecaman$ nats), yet it is the only type that is \emph{under}confident:
optimal $T=\scSurvOptTRecaman<1$, so \emph{sharpening} helps
($t=\scSurvGainTRecaman$). The predictable structure (delimiters, digit runs)
warrants \emph{more} confidence than the model gives, while the rare jumps it cannot
anticipate pull the other way. Still, the gain is only $\scSurvGainRecaman$ nats.

\paragraph{Consequence.}
With the global-temperature gain $\le\scSurvGainRecaman$ nats on real text, there is
no meaningful temperature miscalibration for a per-token adaptive scheme to exploit:
the premise behind an online temperature calibrator on natural text does not hold for
this model. Modern LLMs appear temperature-robust even far out of distribution. We
confirmed this directly on the two most mis-temperatured types (shuffled WikiText and
Recam\'an) with a within-passage cross-validated gate: a per-passage temperature fit
on the first half of a passage does \emph{not} beat the single global temperature on
the second half (it overfits --- on Recam\'an the per-passage optimum swings wildly
across segments but does not transfer), and the causal faithful filter captures no
significant gain. The per-passage heterogeneity an adaptive calibrator would need is
absent.

\section{The form of the belief: not a fast entropy read-out}\label{sec:form}
% =====================================================================
If the model has a belief about $\beta^{\mathrm{dec}}$, does it show up as
pre-decoding entropy that snaps to a new sampler within a few dozen tokens? We
self-generate $150$ tokens at three $\beta^{\mathrm{dec}}$ and read the
pre-decoding entropy $H(\mathrm{softmax}(\ell_t))$ in a late window. The raw
effect is enormous: late-window $H$ moves from $\expcLateTwo$ nats at
$\beta^{\mathrm{dec}}=2.0$ to $\expcLateHalf$ nats at $\beta^{\mathrm{dec}}=0.5$
--- a paired spread of $\expcSpread$ nats ($t=\expcSpreadT$, all $32$ prompts
ordering the same way; Table~\ref{tab:expc-late}, Fig.~\ref{fig:expc-traj}).

\paragraph{But it is garbage-in, garbage-out --- and the swap control proves it.}
Natural high-entropy human writing sits far below $9$ nats, and the
$\beta=0.5$ generations are token salad within $\sim 20$ steps: the entropy
climbs because each random token makes the context less parseable, not
(necessarily) because the model believes the decoder is hot. To separate
decoder-belief from content quality, we hold the prefix's regime fixed and
\emph{switch} the sampler at step $\expcSwitchStep$. If the belief were a fast
posterior on recent tokens, switching $0.5\to 1.0$ should pull late entropy back

Table~\ref{tab:expc-swap}, Fig.~\ref{fig:expc-swap}). Switching $0.5\to 2.0$
\emph{does} recover coherence, but only because $\beta=2$ forces near-top-1
selection that re-anchors the context to in-distribution English --- the sampler
overwriting the bad prefix, not the model updating a belief.

context contains. The belief operates on slower, more aggregate features ---
rank statistics, support shape, blacklist signatures --- not instantaneous
entropy.

\section{Belief-update dynamics: closed-loop vs.\ invariance}\label{sec:dynamics}
% =====================================================================
Finally we ask which $(\gamma,\alpha,p)$ best parameterize the belief dynamics,
using two complementary criteria. \emph{Closed-loop badness} self-generates
under the corrected sampler and penalizes a corrector that fails to track
$\beta^{\mathrm{target}}$. \emph{Belief invariance} teacher-forces the filter on
a held-out natural-text continuation after a prefix sampled at varying
$\beta^{\mathrm{prefix}}$, and asks that the corrected per-token cross-entropy be
\emph{invariant} to the prefix (a well-calibrated corrector should cancel the
prefix imprint and leave the natural distribution untouched).

The two criteria disagree. By closed-loop badness, full correction wins: the
default $(\gamma=1,\alpha=1,p=1)$ is best (badness $\bsweepBestBadness$;
Table~\ref{tab:bsweep}). By invariance, full correction at $\gamma=1$ is
catastrophic: cross-entropy on $\beta^{\mathrm{prefix}}=0.5$ continuations
explodes from $\binvCeFloor$ nats at $p=0$ to $\binvCeExplode$ nats at $p=1$
(near-uniform over the vocabulary), and the only configurations that stay near
the no-correction floor have $\gamma<1$ (Table~\ref{tab:binv},
Fig.~\ref{fig:binv}).

\paragraph{Resolving the disagreement.} Closed-loop badness is a
self-consistency illusion: the filter accumulates a biased $\hat\beta$ from the
corrected sampler's own output, then divides the next logits by that same biased
$\hat\beta$, so the two biases cancel \emph{inside the loop}. ``The sampler's
output looks orderly'' is necessary but not sufficient for ``the filter modeled
the belief.'' Breaking the loop by teacher-forcing on natural text removes the
cancellation, and the bias appears as enormous cross-entropy. Invariance is the
more direct criterion, because it grounds the corrector on data the model did
not generate. Memory decay $\gamma<1$ is the load-bearing parameter: it bounds
the filter's worst-case bias by the fading window's effective sample size,
holding the CE variance to $\le\binvVarCap$ nats$^2$ across the entire $p$ range
(versus the floor $\binvVarFloor$). The evidence weight $\alpha$ matters little.
A reasonable default for off-temperature generation
($\sigma_0=\binvSigmaZero$) is moderate decay with partial correction, e.g.\
$(\gamma=0.95,\,\alpha=1.0,\,p=0.5)$; for natural generation
($\beta^{\mathrm{target}}=1$) no correction is best, since the model is already
calibrated.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}\label{sec:limits}
% =====================================================================
\paragraph{Limitations.} All results use one model (Qwen-2.5-3B), one
tokenization, and a small set of corpora; the ``Qwen is well-calibrated''
finding and the magnitude of the WritingPrompts offset are model- and
corpus-specific. The imprint is only approximately multiplicative
($R=\fzeroRfinal$ off-axis), and no scalar-$\beta$ correction can cancel
non-scalar structure (mode collapse, repetition, vocabulary narrowing); we have
not measured how much of the prefix imprint is non-scalar. The invariance test
uses only $\beta^{\mathrm{target}}=1$, the trivially-calibrated case, because
there is no natural-text ground truth at other targets. Exp C cannot fully
separate decoder-belief from garbage-in/garbage-out, because $\beta=0.5$
decoheres the prefix faster than any belief update could compensate.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Language models do infer features of their own
decoder from context (blacklists, top-$k$ cliffs, an over-confidence offset on
out-of-domain text). A streaming Laplace filter that models this as a scalar
inverse-temperature belief yields a corrector that works in the boredom regime
and overshoots in the confusion regime. The filter is not online Guo
temperature scaling --- it has a different fixed point and hurts as a calibrator
--- and the belief it measures is not a fast entropy read-out but something
slower and more structural. The right way to tune the corrector is by invariance
on held-out natural text, not closed-loop stability, and that criterion makes a
fading memory ($\gamma<1$) essential. The belief-imprint hypothesis is
sharpened, not refuted; the open theoretical question is a closed-loop
fixed-point analysis of the coupled $(\beta^{\mathrm{model}}_t,\hat\beta_t)$
dynamics that would explain the confusion-regime overshoot.
